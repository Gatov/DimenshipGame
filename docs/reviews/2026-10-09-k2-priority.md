# K2 — Priority in Selection, Measured Against the K1 Reference

Date: 2026-10-09. Ticket: K2 (#53) in
`docs/superpowers/plans/2026-09-24-production-scheduling-and-automation.md`. Decision sources:
`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-24-setup-identity-and-interruption-boundary-design.md` (D1,
Decisions 2–4) and `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-24-demand-objectives-and-material-claims-design.md`
(D3: priority tiers, the `Outranked` reason, plans lending priority).

## Goal

Give tasks and plans a priority that ranks ahead of setup preference at D1's safe boundary, and
measure what it buys on the shipped vessel. **The default reports at the end of this document are
the new reference**: they match K1's in every metric, in a report format that gained two columns.

## What was built

- **`Priority`** has four tiers, `Low`, `Normal`, `High` and `Critical`, ranked by declaration
  order and saved by name. Every task starts at `Normal`.
  - `TaskInstance.Priority` is stored on each task.
  - `SimulationEngine.SetPriority(TaskId, …)` sets one task.
  - `SetPriority(PlanId, …)` lends the plan's priority to every task it spawned, at every stage of
    the chain. That is D3's "promotion reaches prerequisites". K6a moves the source of truth onto
    the plan.
  - Both commands emit `PriorityChanged`.
- **Facility selection** (D1, Decision 2).
  - It takes the highest priority among tasks that can start, and runs today's three steps
    unchanged within that tier: current task, then configured schematic, then queue order.
  - A task that cannot start never enters a tier, so an urgent order short of ore does not hold
    the machine.
  - A ready task passed over for a strictly higher priority records `PostponeReason.Outranked`,
    appended last. A higher task that could not start records its own physical reason, so an
    urgent order that is not running says what it lacks.
  - Neither touches the facility's `BlockReason`: the facility is running the winner.
- **The run boundary** (D1, Decision 3). A run in progress, paused for energy, or awaiting deposit
  is never displaced. The selection change lives only where `StepProducer` already reaches
  `SelectAndStart`.
- **Switch-over abandonment** (D1, Decision 4). While a switch-over is under way, a ready task of
  strictly higher priority than its target redirects it:
  - **Retarget**, when the new task is on the target's schematic. The countdown runs on and
    nothing is emitted.
  - **Cancel**, when it is on the setup still loaded. The run starts this tick.
  - **Restart**, otherwise. The full countdown starts again.

  Cancel and restart emit `SwitchOverAbandoned`, with the old target, the new task and the ticks
  that remained.
- **Transport lines** apply the same rule to the free tail slot: highest ready priority, then the
  transfer in hand, then queue order. Cargo already aboard is never touched.
- **Save.** `TaskDto.Priority` is written by name. A save from before K2 loads every task at
  `Normal`.
- **Harness.**
  - A demand takes an optional `priority`, lent to its plan as part of the commit command.
  - The report gains a Priority column on demands and an Abandoned column on changeovers.
  - New scripts `situation-a-priority.json` and `situation-b-priority.json` change exactly one
    demand to `High`.

## Behaviour-neutral at default

D1 requires that a world where nobody set a priority runs exactly as before K2. D3 also says a
ready task passed over at *equal* priority, behind setup preference, records `Outranked`. Doing
that would change task state and the event journal on every ordinary tick, so K2 records
`Outranked` only for a strictly higher priority. The equal-priority explanation is K8's, derived
rather than recorded.

Verified three ways:

- **Replay.** Situations A, B and B-alone reproduce K1's reports in every table. The only
  differences are the new columns and the final hash, which changed because the save now carries
  each task's priority.
- **Journal.** `AtDefaultPriority_TheShippedVesselNeverOutranksOrAbandons` runs a 2,000-tick
  shipped frame plan that does switch over, and asserts no `PostponeOutranked` or
  `SwitchOverAbandoned` ever appears.
- **Suites.** Every existing kernel test passes unchanged.

## What priority buys on the shipped vessel

### Situation B: nothing, and not because of priority

The expedition frames are ready at 1,669 whether ordered `Normal` or `High`, against 1,402 alone.
Tracing the facilities shows why. No frames task ever competes with another task at a selection
boundary:

- The planner put the frames' pressing on Factory Gamma, the least-loaded factory. Gamma's only
  line home is the frames return, at 4 a tick.
- 2,000 components therefore trickle back to the hold for about 500 ticks.
- Meanwhile Factory Alpha, holding the frames' module run, waits on input from tick 740 to 1,243.
- Alone, pressing runs on Factory Alpha, whose return line carries 50 a tick.

The 267 ticks B loses to the expansion are the planner's line choice and the changeovers it
implies, not queue order. Priority acts on selection, and selection was never the bottleneck.

### Situation A: priority costs changeovers and buys no readiness

One demand at a time was raised to `High`:

| Raised to High | Demands ready | Total changeover ticks | Abandoned | Factory Beta changeovers / ticks | Raised demand's readiness |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| none (reference) | 8 / 12 | 2,880 | 0 | 6 / 720 | — |
| expedition 1 frames | 8 / 12 | 3,225 | 7 | 15 / 1,065 | not ready (unchanged) |
| expedition 2 frames | 8 / 12 | 3,105 | 11 | 13 / 665 | 3,122 (unchanged) |
| expedition 3 frames | 7 / 12 | 5,302 | 1 | 26 / 3,022 | not ready (unchanged) |

In no variant does the raised demand finish sooner. In every variant the vessel pays more
switching, and with expedition 3 raised it nearly doubles, while one more demand fails to finish.
The stalls are the hold-stock race the M3 supply rule accepts (two plans counting the same stock),
and priority does not touch allocation.

### The finding: a trickle-fed urgent order makes its facility ping-pong

Factory Beta's 26 switch-overs with expedition 3 raised are not abandonments; only one is. They are
selection, behaving exactly as D1 specifies:

1. The urgent module run takes Beta whenever its input arrives, at the first boundary.
2. Short runs exhaust that input quickly. The urgent task can no longer start, and D1's "a task
   that cannot start does not hold the facility" frees Beta for the outranked `Normal` work. That
   costs a full 120-tick changeover.
3. The next delivery makes the urgent task ready again. It is strictly higher, so it takes Beta at
   the next boundary, for another full changeover.

A tick-by-tick trace of Factory Beta confirms it. From tick 5,004 to the end of the run, Beta
settles into a fixed cycle:

1. One 8-tick urgent module run.
2. A 120-tick switch to pressing.
3. One 8-tick pressing run.
4. A 120-tick switch back.

That is 16 ticks of work in every 256, about 6%. The urgent input for the next run arrives during
each switch away, so the urgent task is ready again at the very next boundary. The one abandonment
is a cancel at tick 4,828, early in the pattern. The same back-and-forth appears once at equal
priority (ticks 2,725–3,165), when the current task runs dry and another ready task takes the
machine. So the mechanism predates K2. What priority adds is that the urgent task always wins the
machine back, which makes it regular.

D1 Decision 4 promises "no automatic flapping", and that holds for *abandonment*: abandonments
are bounded by commands. Selection was not covered by the argument. Here the flapping is driven by
the arrival of material, with no command involved.

This is the mechanism behind the design's own warning (§6 B): "raising only the equipment's
final-task priority changes little". On this vessel it changes little for the urgent order and
costs everything else. What the improved controller in that situation does instead is allocate
material and hold unnecessary work. That is D3's claims and holds (K6b, K6c), and it is not
selection.

## Options for the project owner

Priority as built is D1's contract, faithfully implemented and tested. The ping-pong is a
consequence of the contract, so whether it stays is a design decision:

1. **Keep D1 as is** (recommended for now). Ping-pong is visible: the Abandoned and changeover
   columns, `Outranked` on the passed-over task, and M1's switching category. The cure the design
   names is allocation, which K6b and K6c bring. A player who raises a trickle-fed order and
   watches a factory thrash has learned the lesson the design wants taught ("following
   dependencies, limiting unfinished work").
2. **Amend D1 with setup hysteresis.** Do not start a switch-over *away from* a schematic while a
   strictly higher-priority unfinished task on that schematic has input in flight to the facility
   (cargo on a belt bound for its buffer). That is a narrow, deterministic reservation of the
   facility's *setup*, not of material, and it stops the round trip without idling the machine
   when nothing is coming. D1 placed reservations in D3, so it is an amendment to D1 rather than
   a K2 fix.
3. **Pull K6b and K6c forward** and measure priority again once urgent work can claim its
   material. That answers whether ping-pong survives allocation, which is the question that
   matters for E2.
